Ninaj Awin

Autonomous Drones for Wildfire Detection and Emergency Response

The Eye of Fire

Ninaj Awin was a multidisciplinary research and educational project at the Universidad Mayor de San Simón (UMSS) in Bolivia exploring autonomous drone systems, artificial intelligence and computer vision for early wildfire detection, aerial fire monitoring and emergency response.

The reason

This did not start with a technology. It started with a fire problem.

Every dry season, wildfires threaten Parque Tunari, the ecosystem above Cochabamba and the city below it. Ninaj Awin began with a simple but difficult question: could autonomous aerial agents detect smoke or fire early enough to help people react sooner?

At the Universidad Mayor de San Simón (UMSS), that question became a real multidisciplinary university project. Artificial intelligence, computer vision, automatic control, software engineering, electronics, mathematics, physics and field operations had to meet in the same room — and later outside that room.

We had less than US$4,000. Equipment was shared, repaired, adapted and improvised. We looked for local sponsors and support. We dealt with administrative friction, departmental boundaries, scarce infrastructure and the politics that accompany ambitious work in a resource-constrained public university.

What kept the project moving was not institutional abundance. It was the willingness of students and teachers to make it work anyway.
Before the drones could fly

We had to teach.

Ninaj Awin was not a small research group with a finished skill set. It became a learning environment involving nearly one hundred students and faculty across different activities and departments.

People had to learn computer vision, machine learning, control, electronics, software integration and research practice while the project itself was advancing. Lectures, debugging sessions, hardware work and project meetings often happened in the same spaces.

The educational model was deliberately practical: teach enough theory to build something, then make reality expose what the theory forgot.

From controlled tests to live detection

Testing the perception pipeline live.

The detector was not trained on candles. The perception work used fire imagery in multiple forms, scales and conditions. Candles were useful later as simple, controllable flame sources for live testing: they let the team move targets independently, repeat experiments quickly and see whether the whole perception pipeline behaved as expected.

This particular test became memorable because the system recognized two independent flames simultaneously. It was a modest laboratory setup, but an important engineering step between offline model development and the later aerial fire-detection experiments.

It actually ran

Not slides. Not a simulation. Evidence.

The surviving footage documents an early autonomous drone and computer-vision experiment for wildfire detection. The system processed moving aerial imagery and identified candidate fire regions while the aircraft was observing real fire conditions.

It actually saw fire.

The perception work was not limited to training curves or screenshots. The detector ran on moving imagery and marked candidate fire regions directly in the video stream.

The aerial detections did not appear fully formed. They were built through iteration.

Controlled tests, classifier experiments, debugging and repeated visual checks came before the system was trusted in moving aerial imagery.

And we had to make the aircraft fly

Night stabilization tests

Some engineering nights ended with only a small group still there. The stability and control team kept tuning and testing the aircraft while, in the background, part of the perception team continued working. Test, adjust, fly, fail, repeat. This surviving original recording preserves one of those nights.

From stabilization to autonomy

The first autonomous flight — with a human guard.

Autonomy was introduced cautiously. The aircraft was allowed to execute the control logic while a human remained ready to intervene. The surviving filename itself records the engineering reality: first autonomous flight with human guard.

From university to real users

The project left the laboratory.

The fire department was not an imaginary stakeholder. Firefighters saw the work, discussed it with the students and watched the system being demonstrated. That interaction changed the project: it forced technical ideas to meet operational reality.

The team also tested drones in the context for which the system was intended and communicated the work beyond the university. The project attracted public attention because students were doing something unusual for the time: combining drones, autonomy and visual intelligence around a concrete environmental problem.

When the work became visible

People came to see what the students were building.

Ninaj Awin began in classrooms, improvised workspaces and field tests. As the prototypes became tangible, attention arrived from outside the immediate project team: local television documented the work, university leadership visited, public-sector representatives came to see it, and firefighters engaged directly with the students.

For a project operating with less than US$4,000, this visibility mattered. It gave the students an audience beyond their classrooms and showed that ambitious applied research could emerge from a resource-constrained public university.

But the attention was a consequence, not the objective. The difficult part had already happened before the cameras arrived: teaching, finding components, asking for support, integrating incompatible pieces, repairing what broke, working late and convincing different disciplines to solve one problem together.

What we actually built

A complete experimental chain, from seeing to acting.

Ninaj Awin combined autonomous drone technology, artificial intelligence, computer vision, autonomous agents, flight control and communications into an experimental aerial wildfire-detection system. The surviving repositories reconstruct a distributed prototype rather than a single application.

01Perception

OpenCV, HOG features, SVM models, HSV segmentation and image preprocessing for smoke and fire recognition.

02Autonomous agent

Detection logic connected to behavioral responses and autonomous-control routines.

03Flight control

Multicopter control framework plus Arduino/OpenPilot integration.

04Communication

Image servers, sockets and services connecting sensing, processing and control.

05Human layer

Django/MongoDB, REST services, web/mobile components and augmented-reality concepts for firefighter assistance.

Why 2015 matters

Today, much of this stack can be assembled from mature SDKs, pretrained models, cloud services and inexpensive hardware. In 2015 the pieces were far rougher. Small civilian drones were still relatively novel, autonomous flight was less accessible, modern deep-learning tooling had not yet become standard engineering infrastructure, and integration consumed a large part of the research effort.

A Sunday in Parque Tunari

The system had to work outside the laboratory.

One Sunday, part of the team went into Parque Tunari for a field exercise while other students remained in the laboratory and at urban test points. The objective was not simply to make a drone fly. It was to see whether the whole chain could operate across locations: field acquisition, communications, visual processing, monitoring and coordination.

That division of work mattered. A system intended for wildfire response cannot be validated by one laptop on one table. The field group had to deal with terrain, vegetation, batteries, aircraft, cameras and communications while the remote teams checked that information was arriving and that the rest of the stack behaved as expected.

Parque Tunari gave Ninaj Awin its urgency, but the engineering question was general. The same architecture — aerial observation, early fire/smoke recognition, autonomous behavior and human supervision — is relevant to dry wildland environments elsewhere, including Mediterranean and European regions.

The project was not deployed in Europe. The claim is simpler and more useful: the problem we were trying to solve was not uniquely Bolivian.

What survived

The project ended. Its effects did not.

Ninaj Awin never became a production wildfire-monitoring service. Financial limitations and the difficulty of sustaining a multidisciplinary university effort made that unrealistic.

But production was not the only output. The project generated thesis work, research, reusable software and models, practical experience across departments and direct contact with the difficult gap between an algorithm and an operational system.

More importantly, it gave students a way to experience engineering as something larger than coursework: a real problem, incomplete information, scarce resources, many disciplines, external users and responsibility for making the pieces work together.

This archive exists because a Git repository preserves code, but it does not preserve the people who taught, learned, argued, soldered, debugged, flew, failed, returned the next day and finally made the thing work.
